# Negative Feedback and the Lethality Arc

Read by `/tempo-curve`, `/exploit-hunt`, `/scenario-forge` and `/table-feel`.

Negative feedback is anything that slows a leader or helps a trailing side, so the result stays
open long enough to matter. Positive feedback (a lead that buys more lead) is not a fault in itself
but needs a ceiling or an offset. Every number below is a rule of thumb to test at the table, not a
law.

## Comeback layers

From most visible to least. Hidden forms tend to rate highest with players because nobody feels
helped; visible forms are easiest to tune but most likely to read as charity.

| Layer | What it does | Generic examples |
|---|---|---|
| Visible boost | Openly helps the side behind | Trailing player gets an extra card at round start; the side with fewer pieces chooses initiative |
| Scenario | Objectives and scoring favour late recovery | Central objectives score more in later rounds; ties go to the side that was behind at mid-game; a late objective revealed in round three |
| Activation | Order and tempo favour the side behind | A once-per-round reaction for the trailing side; the outnumbered side activates two pieces in a row once |
| Unit | Individual pieces get stronger when hurt or isolated | A last-piece-standing state with one bonus; a desperation move at half strength (use sparingly) |
| Tempo | High lethality is locked out early | Strongest attacks unavailable until round two; scoring starts in round two or three |
| Resource | Fewer pieces means more to spend per piece | Command tokens fixed per side, so a smaller force spends more on each piece |
| Hidden or environmental | The world damps snowballing without announcing it | Dense cover, short threat ranges, high wound pools, objectives that force a leader to spread out, supply strain after a long advance |

Structural damping also works: fixed, pre-balanced forces plus a short round cap stop list
exploits and stop leads compounding. Exchange results (both sides lose) and supply limits damp
without any explicit catch-up rule.

## The not-charity test

A comeback mechanism should pass all four. Failing two or more usually reads as charity and the
leader resents it.

1. **Transparent and predictable:** players know it exists and can plan around it.
2. **Fits the fiction:** the underdog rallies, the leader overextends, defenders know the ground.
3. **Mild:** it narrows a gap; it does not hand the lead over.
4. **Built in, not bolted on:** it falls out of rules that exist for another reason.

## Lethality arc

For a game of four or five rounds. Shorter games compress the middle rows; longer ones repeat the
crisis with releases between peaks.

| Round | Stretch | Lethality | What should happen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Positioning | Low | Pieces move, threats appear, few losses, no decisive result |
| 2 | First contact | Medium | First exchanges, plans tested, first reserves committed |
| 3 to 4 | Crisis | High | Big swings justified, tipping points crossed, reserves decide |
| Final | Resolution | Settling | Scores settle, the result becomes readable |
| Optional | Last stand | Spike for one side | A dramatic finish for a side that is clearly losing; keep it short |

Rule: the first round must never be the most decisive round.

## Opening damping list

Pick one or two. Stacking many gives a dead first round.

- Range caps in round one.
- Staggered arrivals or reserves entering from round two.
- Scoring from round two or three only.
- Extra cover or protected zones near deployment.
- The biggest abilities locked until round two.
- Hidden or alternating deployment.
- Dense sight-blocking terrain near deployment zones.
- No move-and-attack combination in round one.
- First seat compensated (extra resource, later seats choose order).

## Spike caps

A spike is one result that moves the game far more than the risk taken. Caps:

- Criticals add a fixed amount instead of multiplying.
- Criticals need a token spent earlier, so they are planned, not lucky.
- A minimum steady result on every attack or action, so bad rolls are not zero.
- Higher base defence, so the top end of the curve is rarer.
- Chain triggers capped at one per turn, or each step visible and answerable.
- Swing proportional to risk: a cautious action has a narrow result range, a reckless one a wide range.

## Small-format volatility

When each piece is a large share of a small force (roughly under ten pieces a side, a rule of
thumb), every loss, first strike, extra activation and lucky critical is amplified, and snowballing
arrives faster. Small games therefore need heavier correction than large ones:

- Prefer hidden and environmental layers; there is little room to absorb visible swings.
- Cap spikes harder and raise base durability.
- Limit consecutive activations to two.
- Keep the round cap short, so a compounding lead has less time to compound.
- Test first seat at every count; it matters more as forces shrink.
